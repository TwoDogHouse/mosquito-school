St. George Island, Maryland • Backyard citizen science

We built some mosquito buckets.
Then we started asking questions.

Real buckets, real mosquitoes, questionable smells, and careful notes. We’re testing what happens when ordinary homeowners use Bti-treated standing water as a mosquito-control tool around the places where we actually live.

🪣 8 buckets under observation 🦟 2 different bucket recipes 🔬 Weekly field observations 📍 St. George Island
Our premise

Make an attractive nursery that never graduates mosquitoes.

A dunk bucket is not supposed to repel a female mosquito. The hope is that she chooses the water as a place to lay eggs, while Bti in the water kills feeding larvae before they become biting adults.

The important caveat

This is not a laboratory.

Dogs knock buckets over. Shade changes. Rain happens. Recipes differ. We record those events instead of pretending they didn’t happen.

The goal is useful backyard evidence, not a claim that we have run a controlled academic trial.

The field setup

Two recipes are now running side by side.

Pink group

Six original property buckets

Installed August 7. Pink buckets with perforated lids, water, a modest amount of leaves from the same source tree, and one whole mosquito dunk each.

We have since learned that a whole dunk is more than the current EPA label calls for at this tiny surface area. That becomes part of the experiment, not something we erase from the record.

Blue group

Two Community Center buckets

Installed August 22 at 16416 Thomas Road. Each uses a blue bucket, only four lid holes, about ¼ dunk, and a heavy load of fresh marsh vegetation.

After seven days these were distinctly scummy, murky and smelly, a dramatic contrast with the gentler pink-bucket infusion.
